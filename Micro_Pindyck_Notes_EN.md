# CHAPTER 2 — The Basics of Supply and Demand

---

## 2.1 The Demand Curve

> **Definition — Demand:** The quantity of a good that consumers are **willing and able** to buy at each possible price, holding all other factors constant.

> **Definition — Demand Curve:** A graphical representation of the relationship between price (P) and quantity demanded (Q_d). It shows, for each price, the maximum quantity consumers wish to purchase — or equivalently, the maximum price any consumer is willing to pay for each unit.

**Law of Demand:** As price rises, quantity demanded falls (inverse relationship). The demand curve slopes downward.

**Two underlying reasons:**
- **Substitution effect:** When the price of a good rises, consumers switch to cheaper alternatives (e.g., if beef gets expensive, buy chicken instead)
- **Income effect:** When price rises, real purchasing power falls, so consumers buy less of the (normal) good

**Q_d = f(P)** — ceteris paribus ("all else equal")

---

### Shifts in Demand vs Movement Along the Curve

> **Definition — Change in Quantity Demanded:** A **movement along** the demand curve caused by a change in the **price of the good itself**. The curve does not move.

> **Definition — Change in Demand:** A **shift of the entire demand curve** caused by a change in anything other than the good's own price.

**What shifts the demand curve?**

| Factor | Direction of Shift | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ↑ Consumer income (normal good) | Right (↑ demand) | People earn more → buy more cars |
| ↑ Consumer income (inferior good) | Left (↓ demand) | Richer people stop buying instant noodles |
| ↑ Price of substitute | Right (↑ demand) | Coffee gets expensive → more tea demanded |
| ↑ Price of complement | Left (↓ demand) | Gas gets expensive → fewer SUVs demanded |
| Favorable taste change | Right | Health trend → ↑ demand for organic food |
| ↑ Expected future price | Right | "Prices will rise" → buy more now |
| ↑ Number of consumers | Right | Population growth → ↑ market demand |

> **Definition — Normal Good:** A good for which demand increases when consumer income increases (positive income effect). E.g., restaurant meals, new cars, smartphones.

> **Definition — Inferior Good:** A good for which demand decreases when income increases. As people get richer, they switch away from it. E.g., bus tickets (switch to taxis), instant noodles (switch to restaurant meals).

> **Definition — Substitutes:** Two goods where a rise in the price of one increases demand for the other. They satisfy a similar need. E.g., Pepsi/Coca-Cola, butter/margarine, Uber/taxi.

> **Definition — Complements:** Two goods that are consumed together. A rise in the price of one reduces demand for the other. E.g., coffee/sugar, cars/gasoline, printers/ink cartridges.

---

## 2.2 The Supply Curve

> **Definition — Supply:** The quantity of a good that producers are **willing and able** to sell at each possible price, holding all other factors constant.

> **Definition — Supply Curve:** A graphical representation of the relationship between price (P) and quantity supplied (Q_s). It shows the minimum price at which producers are willing to supply each unit (i.e., the marginal cost of production).

**Law of Supply:** As price rises, quantity supplied rises (positive relationship). The supply curve slopes upward — because at higher prices, existing firms earn more per unit and new firms find it profitable to enter.

**What shifts the supply curve?**

| Factor | Direction of Shift | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ↑ Input cost (wages, materials) | Left (↓ supply) | Oil price rise → ↓ supply of plastics |
| Technology improvement | Right (↑ supply) | New harvesting machine → ↑ wheat supply |
| ↑ Price of alternative output | Left | Corn price rises → farmers grow less soybeans |
| Government subsidy | Right | Subsidy to solar panels → ↑ supply |
| Tax on producers | Left | Tax on cigarettes → ↓ supply |
| ↑ Expected future price | Left (hold back now) | Expects higher price next month → less now |
| Favorable weather | Right (agriculture) | Good harvest season → ↑ crop supply |

---

## 2.3 Market Equilibrium

> **Definition — Market Equilibrium:** The price P* and quantity Q* at which **quantity demanded equals quantity supplied** (Q_d = Q_s). The market "clears" — no unsatisfied buyers or sellers at the equilibrium price.

> **Definition — Surplus (Excess Supply):** When Q_s > Q_d at some price above P*. Producers cannot sell all they want → price falls back to P*.

> **Definition — Shortage (Excess Demand):** When Q_d > Q_s at some price below P*. Consumers cannot buy all they want → price rises back to P*.

This self-correcting mechanism makes competitive markets stable.

**Solving for equilibrium algebraically:**

> 📌 **Example:**  
> Demand: Q_d = 200 − 4P  
> Supply: Q_s = −40 + 6P  
>  
> Set Q_d = Q_s:  
> 200 − 4P = −40 + 6P  
> 240 = 10P  
> **P* = 24**  
> **Q* = 200 − 4(24) = 200 − 96 = 104**  
>  
> Check: Q_s = −40 + 6(24) = −40 + 144 = 104 ✓

**Comparative Statics — Effects of shifts:**

| Event | Curve moves | Effect on P* | Effect on Q* |
|---|---|---|---|
| ↑ demand | D right | ↑ | ↑ |
| ↓ demand | D left | ↓ | ↓ |
| ↑ supply | S right | ↓ | ↑ |
| ↓ supply | S left | ↑ | ↓ |
| ↑ D and ↑ S simultaneously | Both right | Ambiguous | ↑ definitely |
| ↑ D and ↓ S simultaneously | D right, S left | ↑ definitely | Ambiguous |

> 📌 **Real example — Coffee market:**  
> A freeze in Brazil destroys 30% of the coffee crop → S shifts left → P rises sharply. Because demand is inelastic (people need their coffee fix) → Q falls a little, but P rises a lot → coffee farmers' revenues actually increase despite producing less.

## 2.4 Consumer Surplus and Producer Surplus

> **Definition — Consumer Surplus (CS):** The difference between what consumers are **willing to pay** for each unit and what they **actually pay** (market price P*). It measures the net benefit to consumers from participating in the market.

$$CS = \int_0^{Q^*} [D(q) - P^*]\, dq = \text{area below demand curve, above } P^*$$

> **Definition — Producer Surplus (PS):** The difference between the **market price** producers receive and the **minimum price** they would accept (their marginal cost). It measures the net benefit to producers.

$$PS = \int_0^{Q^*} [P^* - S(q)]\, dq = \text{area above supply curve, below } P^*$$

> **Definition — Total Surplus (Social Welfare):** CS + PS. Maximized at the competitive equilibrium — which is why competitive markets are said to be **efficient**.

> 📌 **Example (linear demand and supply):**  
> Demand: Q_d = 100 − 2P → inverse: P = 50 − Q/2  
> Supply: Q_s = −20 + 2P → inverse: P = 10 + Q/2  
> Equilibrium: 100 − 2P = −20 + 2P → P* = 30, Q* = 40  
>  
> CS = ½ × base × height = ½ × 40 × (50 − 30) = ½ × 40 × 20 = **€400**  
> PS = ½ × 40 × (30 − 10) = ½ × 40 × 20 = **€400**  
> Total Surplus = **€800**

## 2.5 Elasticity

### Price Elasticity of Demand (E_P)

> **Definition:** Measures how **sensitive** quantity demanded is to a change in price. It is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.

$$\boxed{E_P = \frac{\% \Delta Q_d}{\% \Delta P} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \cdot \frac{P}{Q}}$$

Always ≤ 0 (inverse relationship); usually written as absolute value |E_P|.

| Value | Term | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| \|E_P\| = 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Quantity doesn't respond at all to price (vertical curve) |
| 0 < \|E_P\| < 1 | Inelastic | Quantity responds less than proportionally |
| \|E_P\| = 1 | Unit elastic | Quantity responds proportionally (TR unchanged) |
| \|E_P\| > 1 | Elastic | Quantity responds more than proportionally |
| \|E_P\| = ∞ | Perfectly elastic | Consumers buy any amount at P, zero at higher P (horizontal curve) |

**Revenue Rule (critical for exams):**

$$TR = P \times Q$$

| Elasticity | ↑ Price effect on TR | ↓ Price effect on TR |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic (\|E_P\| > 1) | TR falls | TR rises |
| Inelastic (\|E_P\| < 1) | TR rises | TR falls |
| Unit elastic (\|E_P\| = 1) | TR unchanged | TR unchanged |

> 📌 **Example — Calculating point elasticity:**  
> Demand: Q = 100 − 5P  
> At P = 10: Q = 100 − 50 = 50  
> ΔQ/ΔP = −5 (slope of demand)  
> E_P = (−5) × (10/50) = −1.0 → **unit elastic** at this point  
>  
> At P = 16: Q = 100 − 80 = 20  
> E_P = (−5) × (16/20) = −4.0 → **elastic**  
>  
> At P = 4: Q = 100 − 20 = 80  
> E_P = (−5) × (4/80) = −0.25 → **inelastic**  
>  
> → On a **linear** demand curve, elasticity varies from point to point!

**Arc Elasticity (between two points):**
$$E_P = \frac{(Q_2 - Q_1)/[(Q_1+Q_2)/2]}{(P_2 - P_1)/[(P_1+P_2)/2]}$$
Used when changes are large (uses midpoint to avoid asymmetry).

> 📌 **Example:**  
> Price rises from 10 to 12, quantity falls from 50 to 40.  
> Arc E_P = [(40−50)/45] / [(12−10)/11] = (−0.222) / (0.182) = **−1.22** (elastic)

**Factors determining elasticity of demand:**
1. **Availability of substitutes** — more substitutes = more elastic (e.g., a specific brand vs gasoline in general)
2. **Time horizon** — longer run = more elastic (habits change, alternatives found)
3. **Necessity vs luxury** — necessities (insulin, water) very inelastic; luxuries more elastic
4. **Market definition breadth** — "food" is inelastic; "chocolate ice cream" is very elastic
5. **Share of budget** — salt (tiny share) extremely inelastic; housing (large share) more elastic

---

### Price Elasticity of Supply (E_S)

> **Definition:** Measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price.

$$E_S = \frac{\% \Delta Q_s}{\% \Delta P} \geq 0$$

**SR supply is more inelastic than LR supply** because:
- In SR, firms face fixed capital — limited ability to change output quickly
- In LR, new firms enter, existing firms expand/upgrade capacity, industry fully adjusts

> 📌 **Petroleum example:**  
> SR: Only pump harder from existing wells → E_S ≈ 0.1–0.4  
> LR: New exploration, new wells, new refineries → E_S ≈ 0.5–1.0

---

### Cross-Price Elasticity (E_XY)

> **Definition:** Measures how the quantity demanded of good X responds to a change in the price of good Y.

$$E_{XY} = \frac{\% \Delta Q_X}{\% \Delta P_Y}$$

- **E_XY > 0:** X and Y are **substitutes** — higher price of Y → consumers buy more X
- **E_XY < 0:** X and Y are **complements** — higher price of Y → consumers buy less X and less Y
- **E_XY ≈ 0:** X and Y are unrelated goods

> 📌 **Example:**  
> Cross-price elasticity of natural gas demand with respect to oil price = +0.5  
> If oil price rises 20% → gas demand rises 10% (gas is a substitute for heating oil)

---

### Income Elasticity (E_I)

> **Definition:** Measures how the quantity demanded of a good responds to a change in consumer income.

$$E_I = \frac{\% \Delta Q}{\% \Delta I}$$

| Value | Good type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| E_I > 1 | Luxury good | Sports cars, fine dining, designer clothing |
| 0 < E_I < 1 | Necessity (normal) | Bread, electricity, basic clothing |
| E_I < 0 | Inferior good | Bus tickets, canned beans, fast fashion |

> 📌 **Example:**  
> Income rises 10%, demand for restaurant meals rises 15% → E_I = 1.5 → **luxury good**  
> Income rises 10%, demand for bus rides falls 5% → E_I = −0.5 → **inferior good**

## 2.6 Short-Run vs Long-Run Elasticity

A fundamental insight: the **same market** looks very different depending on the time frame.

> **Definition — Short Run:** Period short enough that at least one factor of production (typically capital — plants, equipment) cannot be adjusted.

> **Definition — Long Run:** Period long enough for all factors of production, including capital, to be fully adjusted.

**General rule: LR elasticities (both supply and demand) are larger in absolute value than SR elasticities.**

> 📌 **Oil market (most important exam example):**

| | SR Elasticity | LR Elasticity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | ≈ −0.3 to −0.5 | ≈ −0.6 to −1.5 |
| Supply | ≈ 0.1 to 0.4 | ≈ 0.3 to 1.0 |

SR demand is inelastic because:
- Consumers can't quickly replace fuel-inefficient cars
- Industrial equipment locked in for years
- Heating systems can't be swapped overnight

LR demand is more elastic because:
- Consumers buy more fuel-efficient cars
- Industry adopts energy-saving technologies
- Switching from oil heating to gas, heat pumps, etc.
- New energy sources developed

**OPEC implication:** When OPEC cuts production, oil prices spike dramatically in SR (inelastic demand). But the LR response (conservation, alternative energy, more drilling elsewhere) erodes the price increase over time.

> 📌 **Housing market:**  
> SR: demand suddenly rises (population influx) → price jumps sharply (supply completely fixed — buildings take years to build)  
> LR: construction ramps up → supply becomes more elastic → price gradually returns toward the competitive level

## 2.7 Government Price Controls

### Price Ceiling

> **Definition:** A **maximum legally allowed price**, set **below** the market equilibrium P*.

Result: P_ceiling < P* → Q_d > Q_s → **persistent shortage**

**Effects:**
- Consumers want more than producers are willing to supply at this price
- Rationing problems: queues, black markets, under-the-table payments
- Reduced quality (sellers don't need to compete on anything)
- Misallocation: goods go to people who arrive first, not those who value them most
- Long-run: producers exit market or reduce investment → shortage worsens

> 📌 **Rent control (key example from Pindyck):**  
> P_ceiling set below market rent.  
> SR: existing tenants benefit; some shortage.  
> LR: landlords stop maintaining apartments (no competitive pressure); they convert to condos; no new rental construction → dramatic housing shortage worsens over decades.  
> New York and Vienna examples.

> 📌 **Gasoline price ceiling (1970s US oil crisis):**  
> Government capped gas prices → long queues at gas stations → black market → wasted time searching for gas → total cost to consumers (including time cost) often exceeded market price.

### Price Floor

> **Definition:** A **minimum legally allowed price**, set **above** the market equilibrium P*.

Result: P_floor > P* → Q_s > Q_d → **persistent surplus**

**Effects:**
- Producers supply more than consumers wish to buy
- Unsold surplus accumulates
- Government must either buy the surplus or impose production restrictions

> 📌 **Minimum wage:**  
> Set above equilibrium wage → Q_labor_supplied > Q_labor_demanded → unemployment.  
> Workers who keep their jobs are better off; those who lose jobs are worse off.  
> LR: firms automate (substitute capital for labor).

> 📌 **Agricultural price supports (EU Common Agricultural Policy):**  
> Government sets minimum prices for wheat, milk, etc. → massive food surpluses → "butter mountains", "wine lakes" in Europe → government spends billions buying surplus.

## 2.8 Tax Incidence and Elasticity

> **Definition — Tax Incidence:** How the burden of a tax is distributed between buyers and sellers. It depends on **relative elasticities**, not on who legally pays the tax.

A per-unit tax t shifts the supply curve **upward** by t (sellers need a higher price to supply the same quantity).

New equilibrium:
- Buyers pay P_B = P* + buyer's share of tax
- Sellers receive P_S = P* − seller's share of tax
- P_B − P_S = t (the tax wedge)

$$\text{Buyer's share} = \frac{|E_S|}{|E_S| + |E_D|} \times t \qquad \text{Seller's share} = \frac{|E_D|}{|E_S| + |E_D|} \times t$$

**Key results:**
- The **more inelastic** a side is, the **more** of the tax burden it bears
- Perfectly inelastic demand (E_D = 0) → buyers pay 100% of the tax
- Perfectly inelastic supply (E_S = 0) → sellers pay 100% of the tax
- Equal elasticities → 50/50 split

> 📌 **Example — Gasoline tax:**  
> E_D = −0.5 (inelastic), E_S = 1.5  
> Buyer's share = 1.5 / (1.5 + 0.5) = **75%**  
> Seller's share = 0.5 / (1.5 + 0.5) = **25%**  
> A €0.20/litre tax → buyers pay €0.15 more, sellers receive €0.05 less

> **Definition — Deadweight Loss (DWL):** The loss of total surplus caused by a tax (or price control). It represents transactions that would have been mutually beneficial but no longer occur.

$$DWL \approx \frac{1}{2} \times t \times \Delta Q$$

Where ΔQ = reduction in quantity traded due to the tax. Larger DWL when:
- Tax rate t is large
- Both demand and supply are elastic (large ΔQ)

## 6.1 The Production Function

> **Definition — Production Function:** A mathematical relationship that shows the **maximum** output (q) that can be produced from any given combination of inputs, given the available technology.

$$\boxed{q = F(K, L)}$$

Where:
- q = quantity of output
- K = capital (machines, factories, equipment)
- L = labor (workers, hours of work)

The word "maximum" is critical: the production function assumes **technical efficiency** — no waste of inputs. In reality, firms may operate below this frontier.

> **Definition — Technical Efficiency:** A firm is technically efficient if it produces the maximum possible output from its given inputs, or equivalently, uses the minimum inputs to produce a given output. The production function represents the technically efficient frontier.

> **Definition — Short Run (SR):** A time period in which **at least one input is fixed** — it cannot be changed regardless of the level of output. Typically, capital K is the fixed input (you can hire or fire workers quickly, but building a new factory takes years).

> **Definition — Long Run (LR):** A time period long enough so that **all inputs are variable** — the firm can adjust all factors of production, including capital.

> ⚠️ The short run and long run are not defined by specific calendar lengths — they depend on the industry. For a hot dog stand: SR might be a week. For a nuclear power plant: SR might be 10 years.

---

## 6.2 Production in the Short Run (Fixed Capital)

With K fixed, the only variable is L. Output depends only on labor: q = F(L) [K is constant].

### Average Product of Labor (AP_L)

> **Definition:** The **output per worker** — how much each worker produces on average.

$$AP_L = \frac{q}{L}$$

> 📌 If 10 workers produce 200 units → AP_L = 20 units/worker

### Marginal Product of Labor (MP_L)

> **Definition:** The **additional output** produced by hiring one more worker, holding all other inputs (especially K) constant.

$$MP_L = \frac{\Delta q}{\Delta L} = \frac{\partial q}{\partial L}$$

> 📌 If going from 10 to 11 workers increases output from 200 to 215 → MP_L = 15

### Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns

> **Definition:** As more and more of a **variable** input (L) is added to a fixed amount of another input (K), the **marginal product of the variable input eventually falls**.

This is not a theory — it is an empirical observation, and it holds almost universally.

**Why it happens:** With fixed K (fixed number of machines), each additional worker has less capital to work with. Workers start getting in each other's way, waiting for machines, etc.

> 📌 **Agricultural example (Pindyck's classic):**

| L (workers) | q (tons) | MP_L | AP_L |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | — | — |
| 1 | 10 | **10** | 10.0 |
| 2 | 18 | **8** | 9.0 |
| 3 | 24 | **6** | 8.0 |
| 4 | 28 | **4** | 7.0 |
| 5 | 30 | **2** | 6.0 |
| 6 | 30 | **0** | 5.0 |
| 7 | 28 | **−2** | 4.0 |

MP_L is falling from the very first worker (immediate diminishing returns in this example). At L=6, additional workers add zero. At L=7, a worker actually reduces output (too crowded — negative MP).

**Relationship between MP_L and AP_L (always true — a mathematical identity):**

| Condition | What happens | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| MP_L > AP_L | AP_L is **rising** | New exam grade above your GPA → GPA rises |
| MP_L < AP_L | AP_L is **falling** | New exam grade below your GPA → GPA falls |
| MP_L = AP_L | AP_L is at its **maximum** | New grade equals GPA → GPA unchanged |

The MP_L curve crosses the AP_L curve exactly at AP_L's maximum.

### Three Stages of Production

| Stage | L range | MP_L | AP_L | q | Rational? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | 0 to AP max | Positive, AP rising | Rising | Rising | No — could increase AP by adding labor |
| **II** | AP max to MP=0 | Positive but falling | Falling | Rising | **Yes — optimal operating range** |
| III | Beyond MP=0 | **Negative** | Falling | Falling | Never — paying for output reduction |

**Rational firms always operate in Stage II.** Stage I: too little labor relative to capital. Stage III: irrational.

## 6.3 Production in the Long Run

In the LR, both K and L are variable. The firm chooses the **optimal combination** of inputs.

### Isoquant

> **Definition — Isoquant:** A curve showing **all combinations of capital (K) and labor (L) that produce the same level of output** q. "Iso" = same, "quant" = quantity.

Think of it as the production-side equivalent of an indifference curve.

**Properties of isoquants:**
1. **Downward sloping:** To keep q constant when you add L, you must reduce K (and vice versa)
2. **Convex to the origin:** Because of diminishing MRTS — it gets harder to substitute one input for the other
3. **Cannot intersect:** Would imply the same input bundle gives two different output levels — impossible
4. **Higher = more output:** Isoquants further from origin represent higher output levels (q₃ > q₂ > q₁)
5. **Isoquants are dense:** There is a different isoquant for every possible output level

> 📌 **Example — Isoquant for q = 100:**  
> The following (K, L) combinations all produce exactly 100 units:

| K | L | 
|---|---|
| 10 | 2 |
| 6 | 4 |
| 3 | 8 |
| 1.5 | 16 |

Notice K decreases as L increases (you're substituting labor for capital while keeping output constant).

### Marginal Rate of Technical Substitution (MRTS)

> **Definition — MRTS_{LK}:** The rate at which capital (K) can be **substituted** by labor (L) while keeping output constant. It equals the absolute value of the slope of the isoquant.

$$\boxed{MRTS_{LK} = \left|-\frac{\Delta K}{\Delta L}\right| = \frac{MP_L}{MP_K}}$$

**Why MRTS = MP_L / MP_K?**  
Moving along the isoquant (dq = 0):
$$dq = MP_L \cdot dL + MP_K \cdot dK = 0$$
$$\Rightarrow MP_L \cdot dL = -MP_K \cdot dK$$
$$\Rightarrow -\frac{dK}{dL} = \frac{MP_L}{MP_K} = MRTS$$

**Diminishing MRTS:** As you move right along the isoquant (↑L, ↓K):
- MP_L falls (more L with less K → diminishing returns to L)
- MP_K rises (less K → each remaining unit of K is more valuable)
- MRTS = MP_L/MP_K falls → isoquant becomes flatter → **convex shape**

> 📌 **Example with numbers:**

| K | L | MRTS |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 2 | 4 (give up 4K to gain 1L, keep q=100) |
| 6 | 4 | 2 |
| 3 | 8 | 0.75 |
| 1.5 | 16 | 0.25 |

MRTS clearly diminishes → confirms convexity.

## 6.4 Special Production Functions

### 1. Perfect Substitutes (Linear)

$$q = aL + bK$$

- Isoquants are **straight lines** with constant slope = −a/b
- MRTS = a/b = constant (no diminishing MRTS)
- Inputs are perfectly interchangeable at a fixed rate

> 📌 **Example:** A factory can use either machine A (produces 3 units/hour) or machine B (produces 3 units/hour) identically. q = 3L + 3K. MRTS = 1 always. Either use all L or all K (corner solution — whichever is cheaper).

### 2. Fixed Proportions — Leontief

$$q = \min(aL,\, bK)$$

- Isoquants are **L-shaped right angles**
- MRTS is undefined at the corner, 0 along horizontal branch, ∞ along vertical branch
- Inputs **cannot substitute** for each other at all

> 📌 **Example:** A taxi service needs exactly 1 driver per 1 car.  
> q = min(L, K). If L=3, K=5 → q = min(3,5) = **3** (2 extra cars are wasted).  
> Extra capital with no matching labor adds nothing.  
> Optimal: always operate where aL = bK (at the corner of the L).

### 3. Cobb-Douglas (most important for exams)

$$\boxed{q = A \cdot L^\alpha \cdot K^\beta}$$

Where:
- A = total factor productivity (technology level — shifts the whole function)
- α = labor's output elasticity (a 1% ↑ in L raises q by α%)
- β = capital's output elasticity (a 1% ↑ in K raises q by β%)

Properties:
- MRTS = (α/β) · (K/L) — diminishes as L rises/K falls (confirms convexity)
- Flexible: can represent IRS, CRS, or DRS depending on α + β
- Most commonly used production function in empirical economics

> 📌 **Example:** q = 2L^0.5 · K^0.5  
> A = 2, α = 0.5, β = 0.5, α + β = 1 → CRS  
> At L = 4, K = 9: q = 2 × 4^0.5 × 9^0.5 = 2 × 2 × 3 = **12**  
> MP_L = ∂q/∂L = 2 × 0.5 × L^{−0.5} × K^{0.5} = L^{−0.5} × K^{0.5} = √(9/4) = 1.5  
> MP_K = ∂q/∂K = 2 × 0.5 × L^{0.5} × K^{−0.5} = L^{0.5} × K^{−0.5} = √(4/9) = 0.667  
> MRTS = MP_L/MP_K = 1.5/0.667 = **2.25** (or = (α/β)(K/L) = (1)(9/4) = 2.25 ✓)

## 6.5 Returns to Scale

> **Definition — Returns to Scale:** Describes what happens to output when **all inputs are scaled up proportionally** by the same factor t > 1.

Test by computing F(tL, tK) and comparing to t · q = t · F(L, K):

$$F(tL,\, tK) = t^\gamma \cdot q \quad \text{where } \gamma = \alpha + \beta \text{ for Cobb-Douglas}$$

$$\begin{cases} \gamma > 1 & \Rightarrow \text{Increasing Returns to Scale (IRS): doubling inputs more than doubles output} \\ \gamma = 1 & \Rightarrow \text{Constant Returns to Scale (CRS): doubling inputs exactly doubles output} \\ \gamma < 1 & \Rightarrow \text{Decreasing Returns to Scale (DRS): doubling inputs less than doubles output} \end{cases}$$

> **Definition — Increasing Returns to Scale (IRS) / Economies of Scale:** Output rises more than proportionally when all inputs are scaled up. Sources: specialization, indivisibilities in capital, geometric advantages (volume scales as cube, surface as square).

> **Definition — Constant Returns to Scale (CRS):** Output rises proportionally when all inputs are scaled. Represents a "replication" — building a second identical plant. Most neutral baseline assumption.

> **Definition — Decreasing Returns to Scale (DRS) / Diseconomies of Scale:** Output rises less than proportionally. Sources: coordination problems, management overhead, communication failures as organizations grow very large.

> ⚠️ **Critical distinction — Returns to Scale vs Diminishing Marginal Returns:**
> - **Returns to scale** = scale ALL inputs simultaneously (LR concept)
> - **Diminishing marginal returns** = vary ONE input, hold others fixed (SR concept)
> - A function can have **CRS overall** but still show **diminishing MP_L** (e.g., q = L^0.5 K^0.5)

> 📌 **Worked examples with Cobb-Douglas:**

**Example 1 — CRS:** q = L^0.5 · K^0.5 (α + β = 1.0)  
F(2L, 2K) = (2L)^0.5 · (2K)^0.5 = 2^0.5 · L^0.5 · 2^0.5 · K^0.5 = 2^1.0 · q = **2q** ✓ CRS

**Example 2 — IRS:** q = L^0.6 · K^0.6 (α + β = 1.2)  
F(2L, 2K) = (2)^0.6 · L^0.6 · (2)^0.6 · K^0.6 = 2^{1.2} · q ≈ **2.297q** > 2q ✓ IRS

**Example 3 — DRS:** q = L^0.3 · K^0.3 (α + β = 0.6)  
F(2L, 2K) = 2^{0.6} · q ≈ **1.516q** < 2q ✓ DRS

**Example 4 — Non-Cobb-Douglas:**  
q = L + K^2 (not Cobb-Douglas)  
F(2L, 2K) = 2L + (2K)^2 = 2L + 4K^2 ≠ 2(L + K^2) → **depends on L and K values** → non-constant returns

## 7.1 Measuring and Defining Costs

### Accounting Cost vs Economic Cost

> **Definition — Accounting Cost (Explicit Cost):** Actual cash expenditures made by a firm — wages paid to workers, rent paid on buildings, payments for raw materials. These appear in financial statements.

> **Definition — Economic Cost:** The **total opportunity cost** of all resources used in production — including both explicit (cash) costs AND implicit (non-cash) costs. Economic cost is what economists use to assess true profitability.

> **Definition — Implicit Cost:** The opportunity cost of resources the firm **owns and uses itself**, measured by their best alternative use. Not a cash payment, but a real cost nonetheless.

> 📌 **Full example — Owner-entrepreneur:**  
> - Business revenue: €200,000/year  
> - Explicit costs paid: €120,000 (wages to employees, rent, materials)  
> - **Accounting profit = €80,000** ← what the financial statement shows  
>  
> But now consider implicit costs:  
> - Owner could earn €70,000 working for someone else (opportunity cost of time)  
> - Owner invested €150,000 of own savings (could earn 5% = €7,500 in bonds)  
> - **Implicit costs = €77,500**  
>  
> **Economic profit = €200,000 − €120,000 − €77,500 = €2,500**  
> The business is barely worth running economically, even though it looks profitable on paper.

> **Definition — Normal Profit:** Zero economic profit — the firm earns exactly enough to cover all its opportunity costs (explicit + implicit). This is the **minimum return** needed to keep the firm in business long-term. It's not "zero" in the accounting sense.

> **Definition — Economic Profit:** Revenue minus **all** economic costs (including implicit costs). Only if economic profit > 0 is the business truly creating value above the best alternative.

---

### Sunk Cost

> **Definition — Sunk Cost:** A cost that has **already been incurred and cannot be recovered** regardless of any future decision. Sunk costs should be **completely ignored** in future decision-making — only future (avoidable) costs matter.

> 📌 **Example 1 — Factory equipment:**  
> A firm buys a specialized machine for €200,000. It can be resold for €40,000 on the used market.  
> Sunk cost = €160,000 (the unrecoverable portion).  
> The €40,000 resale value IS relevant (it's the opportunity cost of keeping the machine).

> 📌 **Example 2 — Movie ticket:**  
> You paid €15 for a concert ticket (non-refundable). The night of the concert, you feel ill.  
> The €15 is sunk — whether you go or not, it's gone.  
> Decision should only consider: "Is going worth the discomfort?" The ticket price is irrelevant.  
> This mistake — letting sunk costs influence decisions — is the **sunk cost fallacy** (very common in real life).

> 📌 **Example 3 — R&D spending:**  
> A pharmaceutical company has spent €500M developing a drug that just failed clinical trials.  
> The correct decision is: "Is future spending on this drug worth the expected benefit?" Not "We've invested too much to stop."

---

### Fixed vs Variable Costs

> **Definition — Fixed Cost (FC):** A cost that **does not vary with the level of output** — the firm pays it whether it produces 1 unit or 1,000 units (or even zero). FC is unavoidable in the short run.  
> Examples: Rent on factory floor, insurance premiums, salaries of permanent management, loan repayments, property taxes.

> **Definition — Variable Cost (VC):** A cost that **changes with the level of output** — increases as more is produced.  
> Examples: Raw materials, hourly wages, electricity for machinery, packaging, fuel.

> **Definition — Total Cost (TC):** The sum of fixed and variable costs at each output level.

$$TC(q) = FC + VC(q)$$

$$\text{Note: } \frac{dFC}{dq} = 0 \quad \text{(FC never changes with q)}$$

**In the long run, all costs are variable.** There are no fixed inputs — firms can choose any plant size, renegotiate all contracts, and exit the industry. The FC/VC distinction only applies in the SR.

> 📌 **Example distinguishing FC and VC:**  
> A bakery: Monthly rent (€2,000) = FC; flour and eggs = VC; baker's salary (on contract) = FC; delivery driver paid per delivery = VC.

## 7.2 Short-Run Cost Curves

$$TC(q) = FC + VC(q)$$

### Average Cost Measures

$$AFC = \frac{FC}{q} \qquad \text{(always decreasing — fixed cost spread over more units)}$$

$$AVC = \frac{VC(q)}{q} \qquad \text{(typically U-shaped)}$$

$$\boxed{AC = ATC = \frac{TC(q)}{q} = AFC + AVC} \qquad \text{(U-shaped)}$$

> **Definition — Average Fixed Cost (AFC):** Fixed cost per unit of output. Always decreasing in q (the same FC divided among more and more units). Approaches zero as q → ∞. This is why spreading fixed costs over more units ("overhead spreading") improves average cost.

> **Definition — Average Variable Cost (AVC):** Variable cost per unit of output. Usually U-shaped: first falls (due to specialization and more efficient use of variable inputs), then rises (due to diminishing marginal returns).

> **Definition — Average Total Cost (ATC):** Total cost per unit. Also U-shaped, but its minimum occurs at a **higher q** than AVC's minimum, because even after AVC starts rising, the falling AFC still pulls ATC down.

### Marginal Cost (MC)

> **Definition — Marginal Cost:** The additional cost of producing **one more unit** of output. It is the derivative of total (or variable) cost with respect to output.

$$\boxed{MC = \frac{\Delta TC}{\Delta q} = \frac{\Delta VC}{\Delta q} = \frac{dTC}{dq}}$$

Note: FC does NOT appear in MC — fixed costs don't change when you produce more.  
MC is initially U-shaped: falls first (as MP_L rises), then rises (as diminishing returns kick in and MP_L falls).

### Key MC-Average Cost Relationships (memorize these!):

| Condition | Implication | Intuition |
|---|---|---|
| MC < AVC | AVC is **falling** | Each new unit costs less than the average VC → pulls it down |
| MC = AVC | AVC is at its **minimum** | MC crosses AVC from below at AVC's lowest point |
| MC > AVC | AVC is **rising** | Each new unit costs more than average VC → pulls it up |
| MC < ATC | ATC is **falling** | Same logic |
| MC = ATC | ATC is at its **minimum** | MC crosses ATC from below at ATC's lowest point |
| MC > ATC | ATC is **rising** | |

The MC curve always passes through the **minimum points** of both AVC and ATC curves, from below.

### Relationship Between MC and MP_L

$$\boxed{MC = \frac{w}{MP_L}}$$

**Derivation:** To produce Δq more units, need ΔL more workers where ΔL = Δq / MP_L.  
Cost of ΔL workers = w · ΔL = w · (Δq/MP_L)  
MC = cost per extra unit = w / MP_L

**Implication:** MC and MP_L move in **opposite directions**:
- MP_L rising → MC falling
- MP_L at maximum → MC at **minimum**
- MP_L falling → MC rising

> 📌 **Full Numerical Example:**

| q | FC | VC | TC | MC | ATC | AVC | AFC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 60 | 0 | 60 | — | — | — | — |
| 1 | 60 | 30 | 90 | **30** | 90.0 | 30.0 | 60.0 |
| 2 | 60 | 50 | 110 | **20** | 55.0 | 25.0 | 30.0 |
| 3 | 60 | 65 | 125 | **15** | 41.7 | 21.7 | 20.0 |
| 4 | 60 | 85 | 145 | **20** | 36.3 | 21.3 | 15.0 |
| 5 | 60 | 115 | 175 | **30** | 35.0 | 23.0 | 12.0 |
| 6 | 60 | 155 | 215 | **40** | 35.8 | 25.8 | 10.0 |
| 7 | 60 | 210 | 270 | **55** | 38.6 | 30.0 | 8.6 |

Observations:
- MC falls from 30 to 15 (q=1 to q=3), then rises — U-shaped
- AVC minimum at q=4 (AVC = 21.3), where MC(3→4) = 20 crosses AVC from below
- ATC minimum at q=5 (ATC = 35.0), where MC(4→5) = 30 just equals ATC
- AFC always falling: 60, 30, 20, 15...
- TC rises at an increasing rate once MC turns up

## 7.3 Long-Run Cost Minimization

In the LR, the firm can choose both K and L to minimize the cost of producing a target output q.

**The optimization problem:**

$$\min_{K, L} \quad TC = wL + rK \quad \text{subject to} \quad F(K, L) = q$$

Where w = wage rate, r = rental rate of capital (user cost of capital).

### Isocost Line

> **Definition — Isocost Line:** All combinations of (K, L) that cost the **same total expenditure** C. It is the budget constraint for the firm in input space.

$$C = wL + rK \quad \Rightarrow \quad K = \frac{C}{r} - \frac{w}{r}L$$

- Y-intercept: C/r (if all money spent on K)
- X-intercept: C/w (if all money spent on L)
- Slope: **−w/r** (the ratio of input prices)

Moving to a higher isocost line = higher total cost. The goal is to reach the target isoquant with the lowest possible isocost line.

### The Cost-Minimizing Condition

The optimum is where the **isoquant is tangent to the isocost line** — the point where the slopes are equal:

$$\text{Slope of isoquant} = \text{Slope of isocost}$$
$$MRTS_{LK} = \frac{w}{r}$$
$$\frac{MP_L}{MP_K} = \frac{w}{r}$$

Rearranging: $\boxed{\frac{MP_L}{w} = \frac{MP_K}{r}}$

> **Intuition:** The "bang per buck" from each input must be equal. If MP_L/w > MP_K/r, spending one more euro on L gives more output than spending it on K → shift spending toward L until equality is restored.

> 📌 **Example:**  
> Production function: q = L^0.5 · K^0.5, target q = 10, w = 2, r = 8  
>  
> Step 1 — Cost-minimization condition: MP_L/w = MP_K/r  
> MP_L = 0.5 · L^{−0.5} · K^{0.5} = 0.5K^{0.5}/L^{0.5}  
> MP_K = 0.5 · L^{0.5} · K^{−0.5} = 0.5L^{0.5}/K^{0.5}  
>  
> Condition: (0.5K/L)^{0.5} / 2 = (0.5L/K)^{0.5} / 8 → K/L = w/r · (α/β) ... or using MRTS:  
> MRTS = K/L = w/r · (α/β) = (2/8) × (0.5/0.5) = **0.25** → K = 0.25L  
>  
> Step 2 — Use production constraint: q = L^0.5 · (0.25L)^0.5 = L · 0.5 = 10 → **L* = 20**  
> K* = 0.25 × 20 = **5**  
>  
> TC = wL + rK = 2(20) + 8(5) = 40 + 40 = **€80**

### Expansion Path

> **Definition — Expansion Path:** The locus of cost-minimizing input combinations (K*, L*) as output q increases, holding input prices (w, r) constant.

- For CRS Cobb-Douglas: expansion path is a **straight line through the origin** (K/L ratio is constant)
- For general production functions: can be curved
- The expansion path connects all tangency points between isoquants and isocost lines

**Important:** In the SR, capital is fixed, so the firm cannot reach the cost-minimizing point on the expansion path (unless it happens to be at the right K already). **SR total cost ≥ LR total cost** for the same output level (the firm is constrained).

## 7.4 Long-Run Average and Marginal Cost

### Long-Run Average Cost (LRAC)

> **Definition — LRAC:** The minimum cost per unit of output when the firm can fully optimize all inputs (including capital stock). It is the **envelope** of all short-run ATC curves — at each output level, it picks the lowest ATC achievable by choosing the optimal plant size.

**Shape of LRAC:**
- Initially **decreasing** → economies of scale (larger plants are more efficient)
- A **flat region** (or single point) → constant returns to scale
- Eventually **increasing** → diseconomies of scale (management problems at large scale)

The LRAC curve is always **below or equal to** every SRATC curve — the firm does at least as well in the LR.

### Economies and Diseconomies of Scale

> **Definition — Economies of Scale:** A situation where LRAC **decreases** as output increases. Doubling output costs less than double. Equivalently: LRMC < LRAC.

Sources of economies of scale:
- **Specialization:** workers and machines become more specialized and efficient
- **Indivisibilities:** some inputs (like a large server or a blast furnace) can't be scaled down — large output spreads this fixed investment
- **Geometric advantages:** for containers/pipelines, volume (output) grows as cube while material cost grows as square
- **Bulk purchasing:** discounts on large input orders

> **Definition — Diseconomies of Scale:** LRAC **increases** as output grows. Doubling output costs more than double. LRMC > LRAC.

Sources of diseconomies of scale:
- Management complexity and bureaucracy in huge organizations
- Communication failures (messages distorted through layers of hierarchy)
- Worker motivation/monitoring problems at large scale
- Geographic constraints (need to source inputs from farther away)

### Output Elasticity of Cost (Scale Economies Measure)

$$\boxed{E_C = \frac{\% \Delta TC}{\% \Delta q} = \frac{dTC/TC}{dq/q} = \frac{MC}{AC}}$$

| E_C | Interpretation | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| E_C < 1 | MC < AC → LRAC falling | Economies of scale (IRS) |
| E_C = 1 | MC = AC → LRAC flat | Constant returns |
| E_C > 1 | MC > AC → LRAC rising | Diseconomies of scale (DRS) |

### Minimum Efficient Scale (MES)

> **Definition — Minimum Efficient Scale (MES):** The **smallest output level** at which a firm's long-run average cost reaches its minimum. Below MES, the firm hasn't yet exhausted economies of scale.

MES is crucial for understanding market structure:
- **MES is large relative to market demand:** only a few firms (or even one) can achieve minimum cost → industry will be **highly concentrated** or a **natural monopoly** (e.g., electricity distribution, water supply)
- **MES is small relative to market demand:** many firms can co-exist at minimum cost → **competitive industry** (e.g., barbershops, small restaurants)

> 📌 **Example:** If the total market for electricity in a city is 1 billion kWh/year, and MES for a power plant is 800 million kWh/year → effectively only one firm can operate at efficient scale → natural monopoly.

### Economies of Scope

> **Definition — Economies of Scope:** Cost savings from producing **two or more products jointly** rather than separately. Different from economies of scale (which is about producing more of one good).

$$C(q_1, q_2) < C(q_1, 0) + C(0, q_2)$$

This condition says: the cost of producing q₁ and q₂ together is less than the cost of producing them separately.

Sources: shared inputs, shared overhead, joint production processes, shared distribution networks.

> 📌 **Example:**  
> A car manufacturer (GM) also produces trucks. Design, engineering, and assembly knowledge is shared → cheaper to produce both than have two separate companies.  
> Airlines: joint production of passenger flights and cargo services → same plane, same flight.

## 8.1 Perfectly Competitive Markets

> **Definition — Perfectly Competitive Market:** A market structure with four conditions:
> 1. **Many buyers and sellers** — each so small that no single agent can influence the market price
> 2. **Homogeneous product** — all units of the good are identical; consumers don't care which firm they buy from
> 3. **Free entry and exit** — firms can enter or leave the industry in the long run without barriers
> 4. **Perfect information** — all buyers and sellers know the prevailing price and product characteristics

> **Definition — Price Taker:** A firm that takes the market price as given — it has no market power to set its own price. If it charges above P, it sells nothing. No reason to charge below P (gives up revenue). The competitive firm's demand curve is horizontal at P.

**Revenue concepts for the competitive firm:**

$$TR = P \cdot q$$

$$AR = \frac{TR}{q} = P \quad \text{(always)}$$

$$MR = \frac{\Delta TR}{\Delta q} = P \quad \text{(always, for a price taker)}$$

So for a competitive firm: **P = MR = AR** — all three are equal and constant.

> 📌 **Real-world examples of competitive markets:**  
> - Agricultural commodities (wheat, corn, soybeans) — many farmers, identical products  
> - Foreign exchange markets — millions of traders, standardized currencies  
> - Stock markets (individual stocks) — standardized assets traded by millions  
> - Fish markets, flower markets — commoditized products, many sellers

---

## 8.2 Profit Maximization

> **Definition — Profit (π):** The difference between total revenue and total economic cost.

$$\boxed{\pi = TR - TC = P \cdot q - TC(q)}$$

**To maximize profit, choose q where:**

$$\frac{d\pi}{dq} = 0 \Rightarrow \frac{dTR}{dq} = \frac{dTC}{dq} \Rightarrow MR = MC$$

Since MR = P for a competitive firm:

$$\boxed{P = MC} \quad \text{(First-Order Condition)}$$

**Second-Order Condition (SOC):** MC must be **increasing** at the optimum.

$$\frac{d^2\pi}{dq^2} < 0 \Rightarrow \frac{dMC}{dq} > \frac{dMR}{dq} = 0 \Rightarrow \text{MC is rising}$$

This ensures we have a maximum (not a minimum) of profit.

**Graphical intuition:**
- If P > MC → producing one more unit adds more to revenue than cost → **increase q**
- If P < MC → last unit costs more than it earns → **decrease q**
- If P = MC → no gain from adjusting → **optimal**

> 📌 **Numerical example 1 (discrete):**

| q | TR (P=15) | TC | MC | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 20 | — | −20 |
| 1 | 15 | 30 | 10 | −15 |
| 2 | 30 | 36 | 6 | −6 |
| 3 | 45 | 40 | 4 | **+5** |
| 4 | 60 | 46 | 6 | **+14** |
| 5 | 75 | 56 | 10 | **+19** |
| 6 | 90 | 72 | 16 | **+18** |
| 7 | 105 | 100 | 28 | +5 |

At q=5: P=15 > MC=10 → still profitable to expand  
At q=6: P=15 < MC=16 → too much  
**Optimal: q*=5 or q*=6** depending on exact MC at midpoint. Here max π = €19 at q=5.

> 📌 **Numerical example 2 (calculus):**  
> TC(q) = 50 + 2q + 0.5q²  
> FC = 50, VC = 2q + 0.5q²  
> MC = dTC/dq = 2 + q  
> Market price: P = 12  
>  
> Set P = MC: 12 = 2 + q → **q* = 10**  
>  
> TR = 12 × 10 = **120**  
> TC = 50 + 20 + 50 = **120**  
> **π = 120 − 120 = 0** (break-even — zero economic profit)  
>  
> Check SOC: dMC/dq = 1 > 0 ✓ (MC is rising — this is indeed a maximum)

## 8.3 Profit, Revenue, and Cost in Practice

**Three possible outcomes at P = MC:**

| Condition | Situation | Action |
|---|---|---|
| P > ATC | **π > 0** (economic profit) | Produce at q* where P = MC |
| P = ATC | **π = 0** (break-even / normal profit) | Produce at q* |
| AVC ≤ P < ATC | **π < 0** (loss, but cover VC) | Still produce (loss < FC) |
| P < AVC | **π < 0** (loss, don't cover VC) | **Shut down** (loss = FC only) |

> 📌 **Loss but still operating (important!)**  
> FC = €100, VC = €60, TR = €80 → π = −€80  
> But if shut down: lose FC = −€100  
> Better to operate (−€80 > −€100). The firm recovers €20 toward FC from operations.

## 8.4 Shutdown Decision

### Short Run (SR) Shutdown Rule

> **Definition — Shutdown Point (SR):** The price at which a firm is indifferent between producing and shutting down — the minimum of AVC. If P falls below this, the firm shuts down in the SR.

**Rule:** Produce if P ≥ AVC_min; Shut down if P < AVC_min.

**Why AVC and not ATC?** In the SR, FC is **sunk** — paid regardless. If TR ≥ VC (i.e., P ≥ AVC), operating is better than shutting down even if you're losing money.

### Long Run (LR) Exit Rule

> **Definition — Break-Even Point:** P = ATC_min → economic profit = 0. Firm earns normal profit (covers all costs including opportunity cost). Below this, firm should exit in the LR.

**Rule:** Stay in market if P ≥ ATC_min; Exit if P < ATC_min.

In the LR, **there are no sunk costs** — all costs are avoidable. So the firm must cover everything to stay.

> 📌 **Summary of firm decisions:**

| Price level | SR decision | LR decision |
|---|---|---|
| P ≥ ATC_min | Operate, earn profit | Stay, may attract entry |
| AVC_min ≤ P < ATC_min | Operate (at a loss) | **Exit** (losses unsustainable) |
| P < AVC_min | **Shut down** | Exit |

---

## 8.5 The Firm's Supply Curve

> **Definition — Firm's Short-Run Supply Curve:** The portion of the firm's **MC curve that lies above AVC_min**. It shows the profit-maximizing output at each price.

$$q^s(P) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } P < AVC_{min} \\ q \text{ such that } P = MC(q) & \text{if } P \geq AVC_{min} \end{cases}$$

The supply curve is **upward sloping** because MC is increasing (law of diminishing returns).

> 📌 **Example:** MC = 2 + q, AVC minimum at q=0 (AVC=2 at q=0 — minimum of AVC is at bottom of U-shape)  
> At P = 5: 5 = 2 + q → q = 3  
> At P = 8: 8 = 2 + q → q = 6  
> At P = 12: q = 10  
> → Supply curve: q = P − 2 (for P ≥ 2)

**Supply curve shifts when:**
- Input prices change (w↑ → MC↑ → supply shifts left)
- Technology improves (MC↓ → supply shifts right)
- Number of firms changes (LR)

---

## 8.6 Market (Industry) Supply

> **Definition — Market Supply Curve:** The horizontal sum of all individual firms' supply curves. It shows the total quantity all firms together supply at each price.

With n identical firms:
$$Q_S(P) = n \cdot q_s(P)$$

With heterogeneous firms: sum up each firm's supply (some firms with higher costs only enter at higher prices — explains upward slope of market supply).

> 📌 **Example:**  
> 100 identical firms, each with supply q = P − 2 (for P ≥ 2)  
> Market supply: Q_S = 100(P − 2) = 100P − 200  
> At P = 5: Q_S = 300  
> At P = 8: Q_S = 600

---

## 8.7 Producer Surplus

> **Definition — Producer Surplus (PS):** The difference between the revenue a firm receives and the minimum revenue it would need to produce that quantity (= variable cost). PS = net benefit to the firm from selling at market price P.

$$\boxed{PS = TR - VC = P \cdot q^* - VC(q^*) = \int_0^{q^*}(P - MC(q))\,dq}$$

**Graphically:** the area above the MC (supply) curve and below market price P, from q=0 to q*.

**Key relationship:**

$$PS = \pi + FC$$

- In SR: PS > π (by the amount of FC)
- In LR (no FC): PS = π

> 📌 **Numerical example:**  
> TC(q) = 30 + 3q + q², FC = 30, VC = 3q + q²  
> MC = 3 + 2q  
> P = 13 → q* = 5 (from 13 = 3 + 2q)  
>  
> TR = 13 × 5 = 65  
> VC = 3(5) + 25 = 40  
> **PS = TR − VC = 65 − 40 = €25**  
>  
> TC = 30 + 40 = 70  
> π = 65 − 70 = −€5  
> Check: PS = π + FC = −5 + 30 = **€25** ✓

---

## 8.8 Short-Run Market Equilibrium

With fixed number of firms n, SR equilibrium: D(P) = n · q_s(P)

**Possible SR outcomes:**
- **π > 0 (P > ATC):** Firms earn economic profit → attractive to new entrants → entry occurs in LR
- **π = 0 (P = ATC):** Break-even → no incentive to enter or exit
- **π < 0, P ≥ AVC:** Firms operate but lose money → exit in LR
- **π < 0, P < AVC:** Firms shut down even in SR

> 📌 **SR demand shock example:**  
> Demand suddenly rises (D shifts right) → P rises → each firm produces more (moves up MC curve) → market Q rises → firms earn positive profit. But new firms can't enter instantly → SR response is limited.

---

## 8.9 Long-Run Market Equilibrium

> **Definition — Long-Run Equilibrium (Competitive):** A market state where:
> 1. Each firm maximizes profit: **P = MC**
> 2. Each firm earns zero economic profit: **P = AC**
> 3. The market clears: **Q_D = Q_S**
>
> Together (1) and (2) imply: **MC = AC**, which occurs at **AC minimum**.

$$\boxed{P^* = MC = AC_{min}} \quad \text{— The Golden Rule of Competitive LR Equilibrium}$$

**The self-correcting entry/exit mechanism:**

If π > 0:
1. New firms enter → market S shifts right → P falls → π falls → eventually π = 0

If π < 0:
1. Firms exit → market S shifts left → P rises → π rises → eventually π = 0

**Efficiency of the LR competitive equilibrium:**
- **Allocative efficiency:** P = MC → price reflects the true marginal cost to society → no overproduction or underproduction
- **Productive efficiency:** P = AC_min → production occurs at the lowest possible cost per unit → no waste

> 📌 **LR adjustment example:**  
> Initial equilibrium: P* = €20 = AC_min = MC (zero profit)  
> Demand increases → P rises to €28 → firms earn positive profit  
> New firms enter → S shifts right → P falls  
> New LR equilibrium: P* returns to €20 (assuming constant cost industry), but Q* is higher (more firms)

---

## 8.10 Long-Run Supply Curve

> **Definition — Long-Run Industry Supply Curve:** Shows the long-run relationship between market price and quantity supplied, after all entry/exit has occurred and firms have fully adjusted.

Its shape depends on how entry affects input prices:

### 1. Constant Cost Industry
**Definition:** Entry of new firms does **not** change input prices — the industry is small relative to input markets.

LR supply curve: **perfectly horizontal (elastic)** at P = AC_min

After any demand shock, P eventually returns to original P* = AC_min.

> 📌 Example: Pencil industry. New pencil firms don't significantly bid up global prices of wood, graphite, or rubber.

### 2. Increasing Cost Industry
**Definition:** Entry of new firms **raises input prices** — the industry is a significant buyer of inputs.

LR supply curve: **upward sloping**

After demand ↑ and entry: new equilibrium has **higher P*** than before (because input prices rose too).

> 📌 Example: Oil services industry — as oil exploration booms, wages of petroleum engineers rise sharply. LR supply slopes up.

### 3. Decreasing Cost Industry
**Definition:** Entry of new firms **lowers input prices** — perhaps because input suppliers also benefit from scale economies.

LR supply curve: **downward sloping** (rare but possible)

> 📌 Example: Early semiconductor industry — as chip production grew, the entire supply chain became more efficient, driving costs down.

## 8.11 Effects of a Tax on a Competitive Market

A **specific (per-unit) tax t** imposed on producers:
- Shifts each firm's MC curve up by t → shifts market supply **left** by t
- Creates a wedge between buyer price (P_B) and seller net price (P_S = P_B − t)

**SR effects:**
- P rises (but by less than t if supply is not perfectly inelastic)
- Q falls
- Existing firms earn lower profits (or losses)
- Consumer surplus falls; producer surplus falls; government collects revenue

**LR effects (constant cost industry):**
- Losses force some firms to exit → S shifts further left
- P rises until P_B − t = AC_min (firms break even again)
- In a constant cost industry: **P rises by exactly t** in the LR (entire tax passed to consumers)
- Q falls permanently (fewer firms in the market)

**Deadweight Loss:**

$$DWL = \frac{1}{2} \times t \times \Delta Q$$

This represents the loss of mutually beneficial trades that no longer occur. DWL is larger when:
- The tax rate t is higher
- Demand or supply (or both) are more elastic (larger ΔQ)

> 📌 **Example:**  
> Before tax: P* = €10, Q* = 1000  
> Tax: t = €2/unit  
> After tax: P_B = €11.50, P_S = €9.50, Q** = 800  
>  
> Government revenue = t × Q** = 2 × 800 = **€1,600**  
> DWL = ½ × 2 × (1000 − 800) = ½ × 2 × 200 = **€200**  
>  
> Consumer surplus loss: (P_B rises from 10 to 11.50) — area = larger than just the tax revenue  
> Producer surplus loss: (P_S falls from 10 to 9.50) — area also sizable  
> Total CS + PS loss = tax revenue (€1,600) + DWL (€200) = €1,800

# KEY FORMULAS & DEFINITIONS — MASTER CHEAT SHEET

## Chapter 2

| Concept | Formula/Definition |
|---|---|
| Demand | Q_d = f(P) — quantity buyers want at each price, ceteris paribus |
| Supply | Q_s = g(P) — quantity sellers offer at each price, ceteris paribus |
| Equilibrium | Q_d = Q_s → market clears |
| Consumer Surplus | CS = area below D curve, above P* |
| Producer Surplus | PS = area above S curve, below P* |
| Price elasticity of demand | $E_P = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \cdot \frac{P}{Q}$ |
| Elastic: \|E_P\|>1 | ↓P → ↑TR; Inelastic: \|E_P\|<1 → ↑P → ↑TR |
| Cross-price elasticity | $E_{XY} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_X}{\%\Delta P_Y}$ — >0 substitutes, <0 complements |
| Income elasticity | $E_I = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta I}$ — >1 luxury, <0 inferior |
| Buyer's tax share | $\frac{|E_S|}{|E_S|+|E_D|} \times t$ |
| Deadweight loss | $DWL = \frac{1}{2} \times t \times \Delta Q$ |

## Chapter 6

| Concept | Formula/Definition |
|---|---|
| Production function | $q = F(K, L)$ — max output from inputs K, L |
| AP_L | $AP_L = q/L$ — average output per worker |
| MP_L | $MP_L = \Delta q / \Delta L$ — output from one more worker |
| Law of diminishing returns | Beyond some L: MP_L falls (with K fixed) |
| MRTS | $MRTS_{LK} = MP_L / MP_K$ = \|slope of isoquant\| |
| Diminishing MRTS | MRTS falls along isoquant → isoquant is convex |
| Cost min condition | $MP_L/w = MP_K/r$ (or equivalently MRTS = w/r) |
| Cobb-Douglas | $q = A L^\alpha K^\beta$; returns to scale: α+β≷1 |
| IRS | F(tK,tL) > tq; α+β > 1 |
| CRS | F(tK,tL) = tq; α+β = 1 |
| DRS | F(tK,tL) < tq; α+β < 1 |

## Chapter 7

| Concept | Formula/Definition |
|---|---|
| Economic cost | Explicit + implicit opportunity costs |
| Sunk cost | Already spent, unrecoverable → **irrelevant for future decisions** |
| Fixed cost | FC: does not vary with q |
| Variable cost | VC: rises with q |
| TC | TC = FC + VC |
| MC | $MC = \Delta TC/\Delta q = \Delta VC/\Delta q$ |
| MC & MP_L | $MC = w/MP_L$ |
| ATC | $ATC = TC/q = AVC + AFC$ |
| MC-ATC rule | MC < ATC → ATC falling; MC = ATC → ATC minimum |
| Isocost | C = wL + rK; slope = −w/r |
| LR cost min | $MP_L/w = MP_K/r$ → MRTS = w/r |
| Scale economies | $E_C = MC/AC$ — <1 EoS; =1 CRS; >1 DiS |
| LRAC | Envelope of SRATC curves |
| MES | Minimum efficient scale — smallest q at LRAC minimum |
| Economies of scope | $C(q_1,q_2) < C(q_1,0)+C(0,q_2)$ |

## Chapter 8

| Concept | Formula/Definition |
|---|---|
